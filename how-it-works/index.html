How it works

Start with one workflow.

We take one recurring piece of work that keeps returning to the owner, understand how it runs today, redesign it and implement the approach that fits. We expand only when the evidence supports it.

Discuss a workflow Explore the example

  1. Understand it.

    Identify what triggers the work, who handles it, where it stalls and what the owner has to check or chase.

    We start from a few real examples and the people who do the work. The goal is an accurate picture of the current process, including where information is missing and where judgment sits with one person.

  2. Redesign it.

    Remove unnecessary steps, clarify responsibility and make the information needed to do the job available to the people who need it.

    We agree up front how success will be measured, so the change can be judged against where things started.

  3. Implement the right approach.

    Choose the combination of people, processes, automation and AI that fits the work.

    Where anything acts on your behalf, agree which actions require human approval, who monitors it and how exceptions will be handled before it goes live.

  4. Check the result.

    Measure whether the improvement reduces the actual burden after review, corrections, supervision and running costs.

    Expand to the next workflow only when the evidence supports it. Where a change adds burden, revise it or keep the existing process.

A decision guide, not a checklist.

When we choose an approach, we work through these options in order. Not every workflow needs every stage, and many stop well before automation.

  1. Eliminate work that doesn’t need to happen.
  2. Simplify what remains.
  3. Standardize where a consistent method helps.
  4. Delegate to the right person, with what they need to own it.
  5. Automate fixed, repeatable steps.
  6. Use agents where interpretation and coordination justify the added complexity.

We audit the business for leverage, not for AI. The technology follows the diagnosis.

Illustrative example · Fictional data

A reminder is not the same as a completed handover.

Illustrative scenario. The records and events below are fictional. They show how we would think about one handover, not a completed customer deployment.

01

The situation

A service business has signed a new project. The kickoff is Thursday. Its handover requirements include a named delivery lead, but the shared checklist has no assignment.

02

What has to be checked

  • Signed scopeThe signed scope confirms the project is agreed.
  • CalendarThe calendar records Thursday’s kickoff.
  • Handover checklistThe handover checklist has no named delivery lead.
  • Team messagesThe available team messages do not confirm the assignment.

Those records establish a missing confirmation. They do not establish that nobody has been assigned elsewhere.

03

What the owner receives

Needs attention

Confirm Thursday’s delivery lead.

The handover record is missing the assignment. Recommend asking the operations lead to confirm it and update the record before the team relies on it.

Prepared messageTo: Operations lead

“Can you confirm who is leading Thursday’s kickoff and add them to the handover record? The scope is signed; the lead assignment is the remaining item to confirm.”

Available decisions: review the sources, edit the proposed message, approve it, or hold the action.

04

What happens after approval

In this example, approval authorizes that internal message. Sending it does not close the requirement. It stays open until the reply arrives and the record is updated.

05

What counts as completion

The operations lead confirms the assignment. The handover checklist names the same person. Whoever owns the check confirms both match and reports:

Delivery lead confirmed. The handover record matches the operations lead’s reply.

That confirms this requirement. It does not claim the kickoff has happened or the entire project is complete.

06

What happens if the answer never arrives?

The requirement stays open. At the agreed checkpoint, it is surfaced with the latest evidence and a proposed follow-up. If access to a source has failed, that is reported as an access problem. An unread source is never treated as checked.

07

Choosing the fix

The improved workflow makes the responsibilities explicit: what information is required, who checks it, what counts as ready and who handles an exception.

That may be solved with a checklist and a named owner, a fixed automation, or an agent where judgment and coordination across systems are needed. We pick the simplest option that reliably works.

The goal is less chasing and a reliable handover, not simply more messages.

From a first conversation to ongoing management.

These are separate steps. Each one beyond the first conversation needs its own agreed scope and terms.

  1. A first conversation.

    You bring one recurring workflow. We discuss how it works today, where it gets stuck and whether there looks to be a worthwhile opportunity to improve it.

    It is a conversation, not a complete audit, and it does not commit either of us to anything further.

  2. A workflow assessment.

    If there is a fit, we map the workflow properly with the people involved: where information lives, how work moves, what brings it back to you and how success will be measured. The outcome is a recommended redesign and approach.

  3. A supervised pilot.

    The agreed change runs on real work with appropriate access. Anything that acts on your behalf does so only within the permissions agreed for that work. We record useful work, errors, unresolved issues and the effort required from you and your team.

  4. Ongoing management, where it is needed.

    Where the result includes automation or an agent that needs to keep running, someone has to be responsible for it: monitoring that it works, maintaining connections and instructions, handling exceptions and reviewing whether it is still helping. Those responsibilities are agreed before live use.

Questions about getting started.

Will this work with our existing tools?

Often the fix doesn’t need new tools at all. Where it does, we would assess the tools and information sources your work depends on. Coverage needs to be tested for your setup; a familiar software name alone does not establish that the required information or actions are accessible.

How long does setup take?

It depends on the workflow, the approach chosen and the access available. A proposed plan should identify those dependencies before a timeline is agreed.

Does the team need to document every process first?

No. A few concrete examples and targeted conversations are a useful starting point. Clear responsibilities and reliable source information still matter; discovery should reveal the gaps that need attention.

What does it cost?

Pricing is not yet published. Any assessment, pilot or ongoing management would be scoped and priced before you decide whether to proceed, including software and AI running costs where they apply.

Is there someone responsible when it goes wrong?

Where we manage something on your behalf, the engagement should identify who monitors it, how problems are raised, how exceptions are handled and what support is included. Those arrangements need to be agreed before live use.

Will you always recommend AI?

No. We audit the business for leverage, not for AI. If removing a step, naming an owner or a simple automation solves the problem, that is the recommendation.
