How it works

A clearer picture. A prepared decision. A checked result.

We’re developing an operating partner around the work that keeps pulling an owner back in: finding the latest information, deciding what happens next and making sure the follow-up reaches a useful outcome.

Here is the experience we are designing and how we would test it in your business.

Let’s talk Explore the example

  1. Start with what matters to the business.

    The agent needs context about your priorities, responsibilities and active work. That means understanding which sources to rely on, what each stage means and who owns the next action.

    The intended result is one coherent operating picture across the agreed scope. Where information is missing or out of date, that uncertainty belongs in the picture too.

  2. Bring the decision with the background already assembled.

    An issue should arrive with a clear explanation: what changed, why it matters, what evidence is available and what action is recommended.

    You should be able to ask a question, adjust the next step or authorize work without piecing the whole situation together yourself. The right channel, on your phone or elsewhere, would be chosen and tested during setup.

  3. Carry the work through to a checked result.

    Once an action is authorized, the work needs an owner, a next checkpoint and a definition of completion. If a reply never arrives or a record does not match, the issue should remain visible.

    Over time, repeated questions and corrections can inform better instructions and process improvements. Changes that affect how your business operates need an agreed review and permission process.

Illustrative example · Fictional data

One handover, followed all the way through.

Illustrative scenario. The records and events below are fictional. They show the intended experience, not a completed customer deployment.

01

The situation

A service business has signed a new project. The kickoff is Thursday. Its handover requirements include a named delivery lead, but the shared checklist has no assignment.

02

What the agent checks

  • Signed scopeThe signed scope confirms the project is agreed.
  • CalendarThe calendar records Thursday’s kickoff.
  • Handover checklistThe handover checklist has no named delivery lead.
  • Team messagesThe available team messages do not confirm the assignment.

Those records establish a missing confirmation. They do not establish that nobody has been assigned elsewhere.

03

What the owner receives

Needs attention

Confirm Thursday’s delivery lead.

The handover record is missing the assignment. Recommend asking the operations lead to confirm it and update the record before the team relies on it.

Prepared messageTo: Operations lead

“Can you confirm who is leading Thursday’s kickoff and add them to the handover record? The scope is signed; the lead assignment is the remaining item to confirm.”

Available decisions: review the sources, edit the proposed message, approve it, or hold the action.

04

What happens after approval

In this example, approval authorizes that internal message. The agent records the action and keeps the handover requirement open while it waits for the reply and record update.

05

What counts as completion

The operations lead confirms the assignment. The handover checklist names the same person. The agent checks both and reports:

Delivery lead confirmed. The handover record matches the operations lead’s reply.

That confirms this requirement. It does not claim the kickoff has happened or the entire project is complete.

06

What happens if the answer never arrives?

The requirement stays open. At the agreed checkpoint, it is surfaced with the latest evidence and a proposed follow-up. If access has failed, that is reported as an access problem; the agent cannot treat an unread source as checked.

That distinction is part of the service we want to build: a useful account of what is known, what has happened and what still needs attention.

How we would get started.

  1. Understand the operating burden.

    Start with a few real examples of work you keep monitoring. Map the wider process with the people involved: where information lives, how work moves and what currently brings it back to you.

    You provide the business context. We investigate the implementation details.

  2. Agree the scope and the rules.

    Define which work the first version covers, which sources it can use, who owns decisions and what counts as a completed outcome. Establish the starting level of owner effort so the pilot has something meaningful to compare against.

  3. Test the experience under supervision.

    Use real examples with appropriate access to check whether the agent understands status, prepares useful actions and handles missing information correctly. Introduce action-taking only within the permissions agreed for that work.

    The first tests should represent the operating partner you need. They are a way to prove the broader experience in stages.

  4. Review value and decide what earns more scope.

    Review useful work, errors, unresolved issues and the effort required from you and your team. Expand when the evidence supports it. Where a workflow adds burden, change it or leave it with the existing process.

From the founder

Why I’m building We Manage Agents.

Too much of running a business happens in someone’s head: the promise to check back, the question waiting on an answer, the detail connecting one team member’s work to another’s.

I’m building We Manage Agents around a practical question: how much of that routine monitoring and coordination can we responsibly take off an owner’s plate?

The ambition is an operating partner that understands the context, brings useful decisions and carries the follow-through. The work now is making that experience dependable and measuring what it gives back.

Andrew Cherry

Questions about getting started.

Will this work with our existing tools?

We would assess the tools and information sources your work depends on. Coverage needs to be tested for your setup; a familiar software name alone does not establish that the required information or actions are accessible.

How long does setup take?

It depends on scope, data access and the work being tested. A proposed plan should identify those dependencies before a timeline is agreed.

Does the team need to document every process first?

No. A few concrete examples and targeted conversations are a useful starting point. Clear responsibilities and reliable source information still matter; discovery should reveal the gaps that need attention.

What does it cost?

Pricing is not yet published. Setup, ongoing management and usage costs need to be explained against an agreed scope before you decide whether to proceed.

Is there someone responsible when it goes wrong?

Ongoing management is central to the proposed service. The engagement should identify who reviews problems, how they are raised and what support is included. Those arrangements need to be agreed before live use.

Start with the work that keeps coming back to you.
