A guide for business owners

What is a managed AI agent?

A managed AI agent is software that can use information and tools to carry out agreed work, with a provider responsible for its setup, maintenance and performance review.

For a business owner, the more useful question is whether an agent is the right answer for a particular workflow at all, and whether it still creates value once supervision and corrections are counted.

This guide explains where agents help, where something simpler is better and what to ask before handing over a workflow.

Start with the work, not the technology.

Consider a customer project moving from sales into delivery. The signed agreement, start date and delivery responsibilities may live in different places. A team member sends a message asking for the missing detail. The message has gone out, but the owner still has to check whether the reply arrived, whether it is complete and whether the next person can act.

A reminder is not the same as a completed handover. An improved workflow makes the responsibilities explicit: what information is required, who checks it, what counts as ready and who handles an exception.

That may be solved with a checklist and clear ownership, a fixed automation, or an agent where judgment and coordination are needed. See the illustrative example in full.

A property transaction creates a similar burden around documents, dates and responsibilities. A consulting engagement may create it around approvals and milestones. The relevant systems, terminology, permissions and completion checks need to be understood for each one.

What can an agent actually do?

Depending on the tools and permissions available, an agent can gather relevant records, prepare a response, update information, initiate an approved action or check what happened afterward.

Its usefulness depends on more than its ability to write convincingly. It needs suitable access, clear instructions and a way to verify the result. If it cannot read a source or confirm an outcome, that limit needs to be visible.

When is an agent the right choice?

An agent may be useful when the work involves interpreting information that arrives in different forms, choosing an appropriate next step or coordinating across several systems and people.

A simpler approach is usually better when:

  • the work doesn’t need to happen at all, or has steps that can be removed;
  • the real problem is that nobody clearly owns the task;
  • the steps are fixed and the rules are clear, so conventional automation will do;
  • the volume is too low to justify setup, supervision and running costs;
  • a mistake would be costly and the work can’t be checked reliably.

A useful decision order is: eliminate, simplify, standardize, delegate, automate, and use agents where justified. It is a guide, not a requirement to take every workflow through every stage.

“Managed” can mean different things.

Some providers manage the technical environment where an agent runs. Others also help define the work, connect business information, maintain instructions and review outcomes.

Those are different responsibilities. A hosted agent does not, by itself, tell you who will check whether your customer handover or follow-up process worked.

For example, Anthropic describes its Managed Agents offering in terms of the agent harness and infrastructure. A managed business service adds an agreed operating role and the people responsible for maintaining it.

When comparing providers, ask them to spell out what they manage and what remains with you.

What does ongoing management involve?

Keeping context useful. Responsibilities, priorities and business rules change. The agent’s instructions and sources need to reflect those changes.

Checking access. A connection can stop working or return incomplete information. Someone needs to distinguish that failure from a genuinely quiet business day.

Reviewing errors. An incorrect recommendation should lead to investigation and an appropriate correction. Repeated errors may indicate that the scope or workflow needs to change.

Testing improvements. A change that works for one example should be checked against other relevant situations before more work depends on it.

Measuring the burden on people. A busy agent can still create too many approvals, interruptions or corrections. Useful management includes that human cost.

The precise responsibilities belong in the service agreement. This list describes what to investigate when evaluating a provider.

What should you retain control over?

You need a clear answer about what information the agent can access, which actions it can take, when it must ask and how work can be paused or corrected.

Routine work may be suitable for standing instructions. Actions involving unusual commitments or exceptions may need a person’s decision. The boundary should match the actual workflow and be tested, rather than assumed from a general promise of human oversight.

Where are the limits?

Agents can misunderstand information, miss relevant context and produce incorrect recommendations. Tools can fail. Business records can conflict. Connecting several systems does not establish complete awareness of a company.

A credible service should make its current coverage and unresolved gaps clear. Important work needs an agreed fallback when information or execution fails.

Is it worth it?

An agent can be busy without being useful. The honest comparison is the effort the workflow took before against the effort it takes now, including the time spent reviewing, approving and correcting the agent’s work, any work shifted onto the team, and software and AI running costs.

Keep one-time setup visible separately. Compare like with like over a stated period, and don’t extrapolate a small sample into a guaranteed saving. If a simpler process would deliver most of the benefit at a fraction of the effort, that is the better answer.

Seven questions to ask a managed-agent provider.

  1. What work is included, and what is outside the current scope?
  2. Which records can the agent actually read and update in our setup?
  3. Which actions require approval, and who can change those permissions?
  4. How will you know whether an action produced the intended result?
  5. What happens when a source is missing, a tool fails or the agent is wrong?
  6. Who maintains it, and how much time will our team spend supervising it?
  7. How will we measure value after counting review and correction time?

Ask to walk through a real example. A provider’s answers should make the next step clearer, including reasons to keep some work with your existing process.
